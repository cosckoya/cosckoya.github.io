# :lucide-castle: Spain & Iberian

Spain is a country of regions, and its folklore is a mosaic. Unlike Greece's unified pantheon, each corner of the Iberian Peninsula kept its own monsters: Galicia has the Santa Compaña and meigas, Asturias the Culebre and Nuberu, Cantabria the Ojáncanu, the Basque Country the Basajaun and Tartalo, Catalonia the Drac and Dip, Andalusia the Sacamantecas, the Canaries the Guayota. The one creature everyone shares is **El Coco** — the bogeyman whose name crossed the Atlantic and became El Cucuy in Latin America. Spain's bestiary is the original source for much of Latin American horror.

!!! tip "Start Here"
    Spanish folklore is oral and regional. The best entry point is the regional tradition nearest you: Galicia (Santa Compaña), Asturias (Culebre), Catalonia (Drac), the Basque Country (Basajaun), or the universal El Coco. For La Llorona, the origin is disputed between Spain and Mexico — see the Latin America bestiary for the other half of that story.

## :material-baby-face: The Universal

### :material-baby-face: El Coco

**Origin:** Spain, pan-Hispanic · **Type:** Bogeyman · **Source:** Children's folklore

El Coco (or Cucuy) is the bogeyman used to frighten children into obedience: a shapeless, all-consuming monster that hides under beds and in closets. The lullaby "Duérmete niño, duérmete ya, que viene el Coco y te comerá" is known across the Spanish-speaking world. El Coco is the monster every Spanish child grew up with, and the root of El Cucuy in Mexico and the U.S. Southwest.

**Where to learn more:** [Wikipedia: Coco (folklore)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coco_(folklore)) · [El Coco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/El_Coco) · [Cucuy in Latin America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cucuy)

### :material-sack: El Hombre del Saco

**Origin:** Spain · **Type:** Child-snatcher · **Source:** Regional folklore

El Hombre del Saco ("the sack man") is a frightening figure who carries children away in a sack — used across Spain, Latin America, and beyond as a warning against wandering off. He has many regional names: El Tío del Saco, el Sacamantecas, el Roba-niños. The sack is the key detail: the monster doesn't kill — it collects. He is the fear of disappearing without a trace.

**Where to learn more:** [Wikipedia: Hombre del Saco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hombre_del_Saco) · [El Sacamantecas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacamantecas) · [Regional variants](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hombre_del_saco)

## :material-ghost: Galicia

### :material-ghost: La Santa Compaña

**Origin:** Galicia (NW Spain) · **Type:** Procession of the dead · **Source:** Oral tradition

The Santa Compaña is a nocturnal procession of the dead — souls in white shrouds carrying candles, led by a living person who is cursed to lead them until they die or find a replacement. Seeing it means death or misfortune. The only protection is drawing a circle on the ground and staying inside it. It is the definitive Galician horror: the village's own dead walking the roads at night.

**Where to learn more:** [Wikipedia: Santa Compaña](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santa_Compa%C3%B1a) · [Galician folklore](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santa_Compa%C3%B1a) · [Galicia Mágica](https://www.galiciamaxica.eu/)

### :material-broom: La Meiga

**Origin:** Galicia · **Type:** Witch · **Source:** Oral tradition

Meigas are Galician witches — often healers and herbalists with knowledge of the supernatural, but in folklore also malevolent beings who curse, steal milk, and fly to sabbaths. The saying "meiga, meiga, que me mira" warns of the evil eye. Unlike the Basque sorgina, the meiga is more ambivalent — feared and consulted in equal measure. Galicia's witch tradition was rich enough to produce its own trials.

**Where to learn more:** [Wikipedia: Meiga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meiga) · [Galician witchcraft](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meiga) · [Galicia Mágica](https://www.galiciamaxica.eu/)

### :material-dog-side: Urco

**Origin:** Galicia · **Type:** Black dog · **Source:** Oral tradition

Urco is a huge black dog, sometimes with horns or carrying a lantern, that appears to warn of death or accompanies the Santa Compaña. The black dog is a pan-European death omen (think Black Shuck in England). The Galician urco is the local variant: an omen of the funeral that will come.

**Where to learn more:** [Wikipedia: Urco](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urco) · [Black dogs in folklore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_dog_(folklore)) · [Galicia Mágica](https://www.galiciamaxica.eu/)

## :material-snake: Asturias

### :material-snake: El Culebre

**Origin:** Asturias (N Spain) · **Type:** Dragon-serpent · **Source:** Oral tradition

The Culebre is an Asturian dragon-serpent with a luminous jewel in its forehead, a creature of the mountains and caves. It breathes fire, can live for centuries, and must be defeated by a hero, often with the help of a clever maiden. The Culebre guards the treasures of the Asturian mountains — and its jewel brings fortune to whoever takes it.

**Where to learn more:** [Wikipedia: Culebre](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culebre) · [Asturian mythology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asturian_mythology) · [The Culebre legend](https://www.asturias.es/)

### :lucide-cloud-lightning: El Nuberu

**Origin:** Asturias · **Type:** Storm giant · **Source:** Oral tradition

El Nuberu is the "cloud man" — a spirit who controls storms, hail, and weather, appearing as a giant with a large hat and beard. He can be benevolent or destructive depending on how he is treated; farmers pray to him for rain. In Cantabria he is known as the Nuberu too. He is the monster as meteorology: the storm given a face.

**Where to learn more:** [Wikipedia: Nuberu](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuberu) · [Asturian weather spirits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asturian_mythology) · [Cantabrian Nuberu](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuberu_(Cantabria))

### :material-water: La Xana

**Origin:** Asturias · **Type:** Water fairy · **Source:** Oral tradition

Xanas are beautiful water fairies who live in springs and rivers, with long golden hair, singing and spinning. They can be kind, rewarding the pure-hearted with gifts, or dangerous, luring men into the water. They are the Asturian lamia — and their legend overlaps with the Basque Lamiak. The xana is the threshold guardian of the Asturian waters.

**Where to learn more:** [Wikipedia: Xana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xana) · [Asturian fairies](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xana_(mitolog%C3%ADa)) · [Xana in Asturian folklore](https://www.asturias.es/)

## :material-axe: Cantabria

### :material-axe: El Ojáncanu

**Origin:** Cantabria (N Spain) · **Type:** Cyclops giant · **Source:** Oral tradition

El Ojáncanu is the Cantabrian cyclops — a giant with one eye, immense strength, and a taste for crushing travelers and stealing livestock. He is the archetypal "giant of the mountain" in Cantabrian folklore, defeated by clever heroes rather than by force. His female counterpart is La Ojáncana. He is the Basque Tartalo's southern cousin — the one-eyed giant of the northern Spanish range.

**Where to learn more:** [Wikipedia: Ojáncanu](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oj%C3%A1ncanu) · [Cantabrian mythology](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitolog%C3%ADa_de_Cantabria) · [Ojáncanu legends](https://www.cantabria.es/)

### :lucide-tree-pine: El Busgosu

**Origin:** Asturias & Cantabria · **Type:** Forest spirit · **Source:** Oral tradition

El Busgosu is a half-man, half-goat forest spirit with mossy hair and a shepherd's staff, protector of the forests and the animals within. He helps lost shepherds and punishes those who harm the woods. He is the northern Spanish Pan — the wild, ambivalent guardian of the green places, feared and respected by woodcutters and hunters.

**Where to learn more:** [Wikipedia: Busgosu](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Busgosu) · [Asturian forest spirits](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Busgosu) · [Busgosu legends](https://www.asturias.es/)

## :lucide-tree-pine: Basque Country (Euskadi)

### :lucide-tree-deciduous: Basajaun

**Origin:** Basque Country · **Type:** Wild man of the forest · **Source:** Oral tradition

Basajaun ("Lord of the Forest") is a huge, hairy wild man who lives in the deep woods, protecting flocks and teaching humans agriculture. He is not hostile — he is the old master of the mountain who shares its secrets. His female counterpart is Basandere. The Basajaun is a "gentle giant" archetype: the forest as guardian rather than threat, a rare benevolent monster.

**Where to learn more:** [Auñamendi: Basajaun](https://aunamendi.eusko-ikaskuntza.eus/en/basajaun/ar-11988/) · [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basajaun) · [Basque Mythology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basque_mythology)

### :lucide-eye: Tartalo

**Origin:** Basque Country · **Type:** One-eyed giant · **Source:** Oral tradition

Tartalo is the Basque cyclops — a one-eyed giant who lives in caves, eats human flesh, and keeps captured herds and people. In the classic tale, a shepherd boy escapes Tartalo's cave by blinding him with a red-hot spit and hiding among the sheep as they pass, the same trick Odysseus used on Polyphemus. Basque tradition may preserve an older version of the myth than Homer's.

**Where to learn more:** [Auñamendi: Tartalo](https://aunamendi.eusko-ikaskuntza.eus/en/tartalo/ar-125228/) · [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tartalo) · [Tartalo in Basque oral tradition](https://www.basquemuseum.eus/)

### :material-snake: Herensuge

**Origin:** Basque Country · **Type:** Dragon-serpent · **Source:** Oral tradition, San Miguel legends

Herensuge is the Basque dragon — a serpent that lives in caves or under mountains, hoards treasure, and must be slain by a hero, often on the feast of San Miguel. Unlike medieval European dragons, the Basque herensuge is usually a giant snake without wings, tied to specific mountains and springs. It is the guardian of the Basque mountains' hidden wealth.

**Where to learn more:** [Auñamendi: Herensuge](https://aunamendi.eusko-ikaskuntza.eus/en/herensuge/ar-59343/) · [Wikipedia: Herensuge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herensuge) · [Erensuge legends](https://www.euskadi.eus/)

### :material-water: Lamiak

**Origin:** Basque Country · **Type:** Water-women · **Source:** Oral tradition

Lamiak are beautiful women with bird or duck feet who live near rivers and springs, combing their golden hair. They seduce men, but they are also spinners and helpers — they can be bargained with. The Basque lamia has a softer reputation than the Greek lamia: she is a threshold spirit of the water, both dangerous and productive.

**Where to learn more:** [Auñamendi: Lamiak](https://aunamendi.eusko-ikaskuntza.eus/en/lamia/ar-84539/) · [Wikipedia: Lamiak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lamiak) · [Basque water spirits](https://www.euskara.euskadi.eus/)

### :lucide-sparkles: Galtzagorriak

**Origin:** Basque Country · **Type:** Household imps · **Source:** Oral tradition

Galtzagorriak ("red-pants") are small imps who serve a witch or a clever master, doing chores and fetching impossible objects. They are the Basque version of the brownie or house-spirit — helpful if treated well, mischievous if offended. In the classic tale, a man gets one to serve him but cannot get rid of it until he tricks it into an impossible task.

**Where to learn more:** [Auñamendi: Galtzagorriak](https://aunamendi.eusko-ikaskuntza.eus/en/galtzagorriak/ar-56360/) · [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galtzagorriak) · [Basque folklore: little people](https://www.euskadi.eus/)

### :lucide-moon-star: Sorginak

**Origin:** Basque Country · **Type:** Witches · **Source:** Oral tradition, witch trials

Sorginak are Basque witches — women who gather at the akelarre (sabbath), fly, and work magic. The Basque Country was the site of one of history's most infamous witch trials: the 1609 Zugarramurdi trials, where hundreds were accused. The sorgina is not always evil — some are healers and protectors of the old ways. The Basque witch is the monster of the Inquisition's imagination, and its victims were often just wise women.

**Where to learn more:** [Auñamendi: Sorginak](https://aunamendi.eusko-ikaskuntza.eus/en/sorgin/ar-109335/) · [Wikipedia: Akelarre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akelarre) · [Zugarramurdi trials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zugarramurdi_witch_trials)

### :material-axe: Jentilak

**Origin:** Basque Country · **Type:** Ancient giants · **Source:** Oral tradition

Jentilak are the pagan giants who lived in the Basque Country before humans — builders of dolmens and megaliths, destroyers of cities, and witnesses to the arrival of Christianity. The last jentil, Olentzero, became the Basque Christmas character: a giant coal-miner who brings gifts. They are the monster as deep history: the memory of a people who built in stone and then vanished.

**Where to learn more:** [Auñamendi: Jentilak](https://aunamendi.eusko-ikaskuntza.eus/en/jentil/ar-64012/) · [Wikipedia: Jentilak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jentilak) · [Olentzero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olentzero)

### :material-fire: Mari

**Origin:** Basque Country · **Type:** Supreme goddess · **Source:** Oral tradition

Mari is not a monster — she is the supreme Basque goddess, ruler of weather, mountains, and the underworld. She lives in caves, rides the storm, and punishes liars and oath-breakers. She is included here because every Basque monster is understood in relation to her: they are her servants, her punishments, or her aspects. Understanding Mari is the key to understanding the Basque bestiary.

**Where to learn more:** [Auñamendi: Mari](https://aunamendi.eusko-ikaskuntza.eus/en/mari/ar-92492/) · [Wikipedia: Mari (goddess)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mari_(goddess)) · [Basque mythology overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basque_mythology)

## :material-fire: Catalonia & Valencia

### :material-fire: El Drac

**Origin:** Catalonia & Valencia · **Type:** Dragon · **Source:** Sant Jordi legend, festa tradition

The Catalan drac is a fire-breathing dragon that appears in the legend of Sant Jordi (Saint George) — the patron saint of Catalonia, whose slaying of the dragon is celebrated every April 23rd with books and roses. The drac also appears in the correfocs ("fire-runs") of Catalan festivals, where papier-mâché dragons spray fireworks at crowds. In Catalan folklore the dragon is both the ancient foe and the centerpiece of celebration.

**Where to learn more:** [Wikipedia: Sant Jordi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_George%27s_Day) · [Correfoc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Correfoc) · [Catalan bestiary](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bestiario_catal%C3%A1n)

### :material-dog-side: El Dip

**Origin:** Catalonia · **Type:** Vampiric dog · **Source:** Oral tradition

El Dip is a black, hairy dog of Catalan folklore that drinks human blood, particularly of those who have made a pact with the devil. It is often depicted with two eyes that glow in the dark, and in the myth it was the dog that tempted Saint Peter — punished to guard the entrance of Montserrat. The dip is Catalonia's vampire, a warning against oaths and dealings with dark powers.

**Where to learn more:** [Wikipedia: Dip (Catalan mythology)](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dip_(mitolog%C3%ADa_catalana)) · [Catalan mythology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catalan_mythology) · [El Dip legends](https://www.montserratvisita.com/)

### :material-cat: La Pesanta

**Origin:** Catalonia · **Type:** Sleep paralysis demon · **Source:** Oral tradition

La Pesanta is a giant black dog (or cat in some versions) that enters houses at night and sits on the chests of sleepers, causing nightmares and crushing sensations — the folk explanation for sleep paralysis. Its name comes from *pesar*, "to weigh." She is the monster of the night, the weight on your chest when you cannot move.

**Where to learn more:** [Wikipedia: Pesanta](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pesanta) · [Sleep paralysis folklore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sleep_paralysis) · [Catalan night spirits](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pesanta)

### :material-axe: El Butoni

**Origin:** Valencia · **Type:** Ogre · **Source:** Oral tradition

El Butoni is a Valencian ogre — a large, hairy, wild man who lives in the mountains and eats children who wander too far. He is the Valencian version of the child-eating giant, used to keep children close to home. In some versions he carries a club and can be outwitted by clever children.

**Where to learn more:** [Wikipedia: Butoni](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Butoni) · [Valencian folklore](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitolog%C3%ADa_valenciana) · [Butoni legends](https://www.valencia.es/)

## :material-sack: Castile & Andalusia

### :material-knife: El Sacamantecas

**Origin:** Andalusia · **Type:** Fat-extractor · **Source:** 19th-century urban legend

El Sacamantecas is a horrifying figure of 19th-century Spanish legend: a man who kidnapped people — especially children — to extract their fat (*manteca*), supposedly for use in medicine, soap, or machinery. The legend is tied to real criminals and serial murderers of the era, giving it a documentary horror. He is the monster of the cities, the fear of the stranger with a knife.

**Where to learn more:** [Wikipedia: Sacamantecas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacamantecas) · [Urban legends of Spain](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacamantecas) · [19th-century Spain folklore](https://www.mecd.gob.es/)

### :material-ghost: La Llorona (Spanish version)

**Origin:** Spain (disputed) · **Type:** Weeping woman · **Source:** Oral tradition

The Spanish La Llorona is a woman who drowned her children and now wanders weeping, searching for them. Whether the legend originated in Spain or Mexico is genuinely disputed — both have ancient versions. The Spanish variants often place her near rivers or at crossroads, and she is a warning about the consequences of cruelty. See the Latin America bestiary for the fullest treatment of this legend.

**Where to learn more:** [Wikipedia: La Llorona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Llorona) · [Spanish origins](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Llorona) · [Latin America: La Llorona](latinoamerica.bestiary.md)

## :material-volcano: Canary Islands

### :material-volcano: Guayota

**Origin:** Canary Islands (Guanche) · **Type:** Volcano demon · **Source:** Guanche mythology

Guayota is the supreme evil god of the Guanches, the original inhabitants of the Canary Islands — a demon who lived inside the volcano Teide on Tenerife. When he captured the sun god Magec, darkness fell; the people prayed to Achamán (the supreme god), who chained Guayota inside Teide. The legend explains both volcanic eruptions and the light of the sun — and it is the only indigenous mythology of the Atlantic islands to survive.

**Where to learn more:** [Wikipedia: Guayota](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guayota) · [Guanche mythology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guanche_mythology) · [Teide legends](https://www.tenerife.es/)

### :material-fire: Tibicena

**Origin:** Canary Islands (Guanche) · **Type:** Demon dogs · **Source:** Guanche mythology

Tibicenas are demon dogs of Guanche mythology — fierce black dogs with burning eyes that serve Guayota, the evil god of Teide. They were believed to live in caves and volcanic tubes, and the Guanches would make offerings to them. They are the Canarian hellhounds: the fire of the volcano given the shape of a dog.

**Where to learn more:** [Wikipedia: Tibicena](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tibicena) · [Guanche mythology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guanche_mythology) · [Tibicena legends](https://www.tenerife.es/)
