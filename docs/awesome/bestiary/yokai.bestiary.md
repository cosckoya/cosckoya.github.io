# :lucide-sparkles: Yokai (Japan)

Yokai — literally "strange apparitions" — are the supernatural creatures of Japanese folklore: spirits, demons, shapeshifters, and oddities that inhabit the edges of the human world. Unlike Western monsters, yokai are not always evil. Many are mischievous, some are protective, others are tragic, and a few are genuinely terrifying. Japan has an entire taxonomy of them, collected in the Edo-period *hyakki yagyo* ("night parade of one hundred demons") scrolls, and catalogued by folklorists like Lafcadio Hearn and Kunio Yanagita.

!!! tip "Start Here"
    The definitive modern reference is **Yokai.com** — a beautifully illustrated database of hundreds of yokai. For the classics, start with the four most famous: Kappa (the river imp), Tengu (the mountain goblin), Oni (the ogre), and Kitsune (the fox). They appear in every era of Japanese culture, from Noh plays to modern anime.

## :material-water: The Water Yokai

### :material-turtle: Kappa

**Origin:** Japan · **Type:** River imp · **Source:** Edo-period bestiaries, oral tradition

The kappa is a green, turtle-like river creature with a water-filled dish on its head — drain the dish and it loses its power. It drags horses and children into rivers, but it is also polite, obsessed with sumo wrestling, and bound by promises. It is the most famous Japanese water yokai, and the subject of hundreds of regional tales — sometimes a killer, sometimes a trickster who can be reasoned with.

**Where to learn more:** [Yokai.com: Kappa](https://yokai.com/kappa/) · [Wikipedia: Kappa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kappa_(folklore)) · [Kappa in modern culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kappa_(folklore)#In_popular_culture)

### :material-fish: Umibōzu

**Origin:** Japan (ocean) · **Type:** Sea monk · **Source:** Oral tradition

Umibōzu is a gigantic black figure that rises from the sea on calm nights, capsizing ships and demanding a barrel to fill with water — if you refuse, it drowns you. Its name means "sea monk," and it is one of the most feared maritime yokai of Japan, described by sailors in countless coastal villages. It is the monster of the deep: the ocean's own terrifying silence given form.

**Where to learn more:** [Yokai.com: Umibōzu](https://yokai.com/umibouzu/) · [Wikipedia: Umibōzu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umib%C5%8Dzu) · [Japanese sea folklore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umib%C5%8Dzu#Legends)

## :lucide-bird: The Mountain Yokai

### :lucide-bird: Tengu

**Origin:** Japan · **Type:** Mountain goblin · **Source:** Heian-period texts, oral tradition

The tengu is a bird-like or red-faced mountain creature with a long nose, carrying a fan or sword, who lives in the deep mountains and teaches martial arts to worthy humans — or punishes the arrogant. Originally a Buddhist-imported demon, the tengu evolved into Japan's most complex yokai: part demon, part mountain spirit, part martial arts master. The *daitengu* are the great ones; the *kotengu* are the lesser crow-tengu.

**Where to learn more:** [Yokai.com: Tengu](https://yokai.com/tengu/) · [Wikipedia: Tengu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tengu) · [Tengu in martial arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tengu#Martial_arts)

### :lucide-mountain: Yamamba

**Origin:** Japan · **Type:** Mountain witch · **Source:** Noh plays, oral tradition

Yamamba is the mountain witch — an old woman who lives deep in the forests, sometimes devouring travelers, sometimes helping lost children. In the Noh play *Yamamba* she is a tragic figure: a creature who must wander the mountains forever, unable to rest. She is the yokai of the forest's ambiguity: the old woman who might save you or eat you.

**Where to learn more:** [Yokai.com: Yamamba](https://yokai.com/yamamba/) · [Wikipedia: Yamamba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yamamba) · [Noh play Yamamba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yamamba_(Noh_play))

## :material-skull: The Demons & Ogres

### :material-skull: Oni

**Origin:** Japan · **Type:** Ogre-demon · **Source:** Heian folklore, Setsubun tradition

The oni is the classic Japanese ogre: a huge, horned, red or blue demon with a tiger-skin loincloth and an iron club, who lives in the mountains or the underworld and devours humans. Every February 3rd, the Setsubun festival drives them out by throwing roasted soybeans and chanting "Oni wa soto! Fuku wa uchi!" ("Out with the demons! In with luck!"). The oni is the monster of the threshold: the danger at the gate that must be ritually expelled.

**Where to learn more:** [Yokai.com: Oni](https://yokai.com/oni/) · [Wikipedia: Oni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oni_(folklore)) · [Setsubun festival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Setsubun)

### :material-bug-outline: Gashadokuro

**Origin:** Japan · **Type:** Giant skeleton · **Source:** Oral tradition

Gashadokuro is a giant skeleton — the size of a house — formed from the accumulated bones of people who died of starvation or in battle, wandering at night and crushing the living, invisible until it attacks. Its name means "starving skeleton." It is one of the most visually iconic yokai, and its image has spread worldwide through Japanese art and media.

**Where to learn more:** [Yokai.com: Gashadokuro](https://yokai.com/gashadokuro/) · [Wikipedia: Gashadokuro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gashadokuro) · [Gashadokuro in media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gashadokuro#In_popular_culture)

## :material-dog-side: The Shapeshifters

### :material-dog-side: Kitsune

**Origin:** Japan · **Type:** Fox spirit · **Source:** Oral tradition, Noh plays

The kitsune is the fox spirit — one of Japan's most complex yokai: a shapeshifter that can take human form (usually a beautiful woman), lives for centuries, and grows more powerful with age, eventually gaining nine tails. Kitsune can be mischievous tricksters, devoted guardians (as in the Inari shrines), or dangerous seductresses. The fox is the monster of deception: the familiar thing that is secretly something else.

**Where to learn more:** [Yokai.com: Kitsune](https://yokai.com/kitsune/) · [Wikipedia: Kitsune](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kitsune) · [Kitsune in Inari worship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inari_%C5%8Ckami)

### :material-rabbit: Tanuki

**Origin:** Japan · **Type:** Raccoon dog trickster · **Source:** Oral tradition, folktales

The tanuki is the Japanese raccoon dog — the beloved trickster yokai that shapeshifts, inflates its scrotum to comical size, and uses a magic leaf to transform. Unlike the kitsune's dangerous cunning, the tanuki is a prankster and glutton, often fooling humans for fun rather than harm. It is the yokai of chaos comedy: the creature that makes you question whether the world is real or just a prank.

**Where to learn more:** [Yokai.com: Tanuki](https://yokai.com/tanuki/) · [Wikipedia: Tanuki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_raccoon_dog#In_folklore) · [Tanuki statues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tanuki#Tanuki_in_folklore)

### :lucide-moon-star: Yuki-onna

**Origin:** Japan · **Type:** Snow woman · **Source:** Lafcadio Hearn, oral tradition

Yuki-onna is the snow woman: a beautiful, pale spirit who appears during snowstorms and kills travelers with her freezing breath — or spares them on a promise they must keep. Lafcadio Hearn's version, in *Kwaidan* (1904), is the classic: a woodcutter spared by the snow woman on condition he never tell anyone, a promise he breaks years later. She is the monster of winter: beauty and death frozen together.

**Where to learn more:** [Yokai.com: Yuki-onna](https://yokai.com/yukionna/) · [Wikipedia: Yuki-onna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yuki-onna) · [Kwaidan by Hearn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kwaidan:_Stories_and_Studies_of_Strange_Things)

## :lucide-ghost: The Spectral & Cursed

### :lucide-ghost: Rokurokubi

**Origin:** Japan · **Type:** Neck-stretching woman · **Source:** Edo-period tales

The rokurokubi appears to be a normal woman by day — but at night her neck stretches to impossible lengths, and her head wanders the house, spying and drinking lamp oil. In some versions she is a living person with a curse; in others, a ghost. She is the monster of the domestic: the wife or servant who is not what she seems when the lights go out.

**Where to learn more:** [Yokai.com: Rokurokubi](https://yokai.com/rokurokubi/) · [Wikipedia: Rokurokubi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rokurokubi) · [Nukekubi variant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nukekubi)

### :material-skull: Kuchisake-onna

**Origin:** Japan (modern) · **Type:** Slit-mouth woman · **Source:** Urban legend, 1979

Kuchisake-onna is a modern urban legend: a woman wearing a surgical mask who asks, "Am I pretty?" If you answer yes, she reveals a slit mouth and asks again — and either way, she kills you or scars you to match. The legend swept Japan in 1979, with schools sending children home escorted, and it resurfaces periodically. She is the yokai of the modern era: the stranger behind the mask, the question with no safe answer.

**Where to learn more:** [Yokai.com: Kuchisake-onna](https://yokai.com/kuchisakeonna/) · [Wikipedia: Kuchisake-onna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuchisake-onna) · [The 1979 panic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuchisake-onna#1979_panic)
